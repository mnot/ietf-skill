# Privacy considerations — RFC 6973

**Text:** `rfc6973.txt`

## Does it fire?

The draft handles data about identifiable people, or emits per-user events that someone other than
the user can observe. "Identifiable" is broader than "identified": a stable pseudonym, a device
identifier, or an address counts.

## Where to look

- **§7** — the questionnaire. §7.1 (what is exposed, and to which entity) is the section most
  reviews end up citing; §7.2 covers user participation, §7.4 covers stating the trade-off.
- **§5.2.1 Correlation** and **§5.2.2 Identification** — when the concern is linkage across
  contexts or over time.
- **§5.2.5 Exclusion** — when the subject cannot see or contest what is held about them.
- **§5.1.1 Surveillance** — when a third party observes rather than participates.
- **§6.1 Data minimisation** — when the design collects more than its function needs.

## What this lens asks

- What does the protocol expose about individuals, their devices, or their use of them?
- To *which* entity is each thing exposed? Name the parties, not "observers".
- Can the subject learn what is held about them, or contest it?
- Does the draft claim a privacy property it has not argued for?
- Where the design trades privacy for something else, is the trade stated?

## Firing, versus only looking like it

A draft that exposes something and says so has engaged this lens. A draft that asserts
"privacy-preserving" or "metadata-only" without argument has not, and that gap is the finding.
Where the draft's own introduction makes a privacy argument against an alternative, the symmetric
analysis of its own design is owed.

## What it does not cover

Mass observation at internet scale — that is RFC 7258, which is a narrower and harder test. Whether
a party learns more than its function needs, framed as a design remedy — that is RFC 9614.
