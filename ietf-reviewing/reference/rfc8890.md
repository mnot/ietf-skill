# End users — RFC 8890

**Text:** `rfc8890.txt`

## Does it fire?

**Rarely, and almost never on an ordinary protocol specification.** This is guidance to the
community on how the IETF weighs competing interests, so a spec puts it at the wrong altitude: what
comes back is a position on the design rather than a defect in the document.

Skip it unless one of these holds:

- the document is a charter, an architecture statement, or a policy document;
- the specification arbitrates between parties whose interests genuinely conflict, and the document
  chooses a winner without saying so;
- the document itself claims to serve a party that the mechanism does not.

Even then, check first whether the actor table and RFC 3552's residual-risk requirement already
carry the point. They usually do, and more concretely.

None of the three holding is enough on its own: rule this lens out unless you can also say what it
would add that RFC 3552 would not. This overrides the general "dispatch when unsure" rule.

## Where to look

- **§4.4** — the one clause that yields a document-level finding: where a trade-off cannot be
  avoided, the choice and the reasoning ought to be documented.
- **§2** — who counts as an end user, when the question is genuinely open.
- **§4.3** — conflicting interests.
- **§4.5** — concentration of power.

## What this lens asks

- Does the document make a trade-off between parties and leave the reasoning unwritten?
- Does it claim to serve a party the mechanism gives nothing to?

## Firing, versus only looking like it

The recurring failure is a claim about the *topic* rather than the *document*. "This design gives
providers too much power" is a position; "the document never states that the provider chooses which
key is served, and never treats that as a risk" is a finding.

It fires misleadingly where the served party and the affected party are the same population, which
is the common case.

If it is not adding something the actor table and RFC 3552 missed, return "does not apply" and say
so.

## What it does not cover

Whether the power asymmetry is *wrong* — the IETF's position is about examining and documenting it,
not forbidding it.
