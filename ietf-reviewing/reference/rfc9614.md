# Partitioning for privacy — RFC 9614

**Text:** `rfc9614.txt`

## Does it fire?

One party in the design learns more than it needs in order to do its job — characteristically, a
single party that holds both *who* the user is and *what* they are doing.

## Where to look

- **§2** — the least-privilege goal, and the framing of separating who from what.
- **§2.1** — privacy contexts, and the rule about a party appearing in more than one.
- **§4, §4.1–4.3** — applying it, and the worked examples. §4.3's DNS-over-HTTPS example is the
  clearest structural analogue for a lookup that reveals both requester and subject.

## What this lens asks

- List each party and what it learns. Does any hold both identity and activity?
- Is that combination needed for the function, or incidental to how the design is arranged?
- Could the two be held by different parties without breaking anything?

## Firing, versus only looking like it

The trigger reads as though it fires on almost any multi-party system, and it nearly does. What
separates a finding from an observation is whether a partitioning remedy is actually available:
recommending an oblivious transport for a deployed single-fetch protocol is redesign, not review.

Where no remedy is available, this lens still earns its place — but the finding is *disclosure*,
not restructuring: the document should state the correlation rather than fix it. Say that
explicitly, or the recipient hears an impossible demand.

## What it does not cover

Whether the data is sensitive (RFC 6973), or whether the observation is at monitoring scale
(RFC 7258). This lens is about the *shape* of who-holds-what.
