# Endpoint assumptions — RFC 3552

**Text:** `rfc3552.txt`

## Does it fire?

Always. Every draft has, or should have, a Security Considerations section, and this is the
document that says what belongs in one.

## Where to look

- **§5** — what a Security Considerations section must contain. The most-used section in this
  lens by a wide margin; start here.
- **§3, §3.1** — the Internet threat model, and the limited threat models that depart from it.
  Read when the draft's trust assumptions are the concern.
- **§4.6** — denial of service, when the draft creates unbounded or amplifying behaviour.
- **§2.1–2.3** — the attack taxonomy, when you need the vocabulary for a specific attack class.

## What this lens asks

- Does the draft say which attacks are in scope and which are out, and why?
- Does it work through the enumerated attack forms, or skip the ones that are awkward?
- Where it relies on a lower layer for its security, does it say what that layer is expected to
  provide and what the combined system then guarantees?
- Is there a single party whose compromise defeats the whole protocol, and is that stated?
- Is the residual risk after mitigation described, or only the mitigation?

## Firing, versus only looking like it

This lens is about the *document*, not the design. A protocol with a weak security posture that
says so clearly passes it; a strong one that leaves its assumptions implicit does not. The finding
is almost always "this is not written down", not "this is wrong".

## What it does not cover

Whether the cryptography is sound, whether the threat model is the right one to have chosen, or
privacy as distinct from security — that is RFC 6973.
